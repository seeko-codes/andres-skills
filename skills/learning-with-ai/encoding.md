# The encoding model

The mechanism behind every rule in [SKILL.md](SKILL.md). Encoding is how information gets into a durable, usable form; everything here is about that first act — not about review schedules, habits, or motivation, which are separate machinery downstream of it.

## 1. Operations, not exposure

Memory and understanding are determined by the cognitive operations performed on information, not by exposure to it. Processing runs on a spectrum:

- **Shallow:** rereading, highlighting, verbatim copying, recognition, memorizing items in isolation, understanding a concept without relating it to anything. Feels straightforward, tedious, safe — and encodes almost nothing durable.
- **Deep:** comparing to find similarities and differences, judging the importance of what was found, producing answers and structures rather than receiving them. Feels effortful, sometimes confusing — and is where encoding actually happens.

Three load-bearing consequences:

- **The asymmetry.** Deep operations produce shallow outcomes for free — you cannot compare two ideas without also ending up knowing what each one is, so the facts come along as a side effect. The reverse never happens: memorizing facts produces no relationships. This is why starting with deep operations is *more* efficient, not a luxury — it shrinks the pile that must later be brute-memorized.
- **Generation.** Attempting to produce an answer or a structure is itself the encoding event, even when the attempt fails or the question goes unanswered. The learning is in the attempt.
- **Noticing is not judging.** Spotting that two things are similar (analysis) is a different and easier act than deciding that the similarity *matters* enough to organize around (evaluation). Most weak structure comes from stopping at the first act. The linear reading of Bloom's taxonomy — secure memory and comprehension first, analyze and evaluate later — has it backwards: memory and comprehension are *outcomes* of the higher operations, and real learning moves between levels constantly within a single pass.

One caveat that matters for teaching: baseline processing depth varies between people. Some learners encode deeply out of habit and perform well with mediocre methods; copying such a person's methods copies nothing, because their methods were never the active ingredient. Habits, not intentions, set a learner's default depth — which is why depth must be forced by the task's design, not requested.

## 2. Knowledge is a network

The brain keeps what is connected and prunes what is isolated. A fact memorized by itself will be forgotten; a concept understood but related to nothing will also be forgotten — "understood" is not a storage class, connected is.

- **Two kinds of connection**, both to be pursued at every opportunity: to *prior knowledge* (analogies, "this is like X I already know," renaming jargon into something intuitive) and *among the new items* (comparing them, grouping them, tracing how one affects another). Working a problem with new material can build both at once.
- **Grouping is compression.** Organizing many items into a few meaningful groups lets the brain track the groups instead of the items, and each group unpacks back down to its contents on demand. This is what makes volume manageable — the alternative is tracking every item individually.
- **Group quality is a scale.** Bottom: grouped by an arbitrary surface feature — a first-letter mnemonic is the canonical floor, memorable short-term and structurally worthless. Middle: grouped for a real reason. Better: the relations *inside* the group made explicit. Best: the structure leans on prior knowledge so it feels logical and barely needs memorizing at all. A good structure also hints at its own missing pieces — from the groups you have, you can half-derive the ones you don't.
- **Direction beats association.** A link that says *what kind* of relationship it is — causes, enables, precedes — encodes more than a bare "related to," because direction must be reasoned about to be asserted. A diagram of undirected lines is compatible with many contradictory readings; it hides exactly the information that was supposed to be learned.
- **The zero-connection diagnostic.** When new material connects to nothing — no analogy lands, no relation among the new items is visible — the material is too detailed for the learner's current frame. The remedy is never to push harder at that level; it is to back up and build a broader, shallower frame the material can attach to.

## 3. Importance is the organizing judgment

Attention, time, and structure are finite; something has to decide what gets them. That something is an explicit judgment of importance — never presented order, never momentum, never comfort.

- **"Why does this matter?" answered properly** yields *multiple* reasons, which are then compared. One reason is a checklist tick; with nothing to compare it against, no judgment happens, and judgment is where structure comes from.
- **Drill past the first reason.** The first answer to "why does it matter" is the immediate reason and usually not the load-bearing one. Keep asking "so what?" of each answer until the question stops making sense; what's left is the fundamental reason, and groups anchored on fundamental reasons are broader, hold more, and connect more easily than groups anchored on immediate ones.
- **What "importance" resolves to is domain-shaped** — function in one field, mechanism in another, the author's intent in a third — which is exactly why the universal question is "why does this matter?" and not any domain shortcut.
- **Structure manufactures relevance.** In any real topic, a modest fraction is concepts and the bulk is detail. How much of that detail *seems* arbitrary depends on the structure above it: with a strong frame, detail becomes meaningful and cheap to keep; without one, the learner drowns in disconnected specifics. When most of a topic looks like arbitrary memorization, the problem is upstream in the frame — the lever is fixing the structure, not memorizing harder. Overinvesting in the top-level structure pays for itself many times over in the detail layers.
- **Circular importance is fake importance.** "A matters because it produces B" where B only matters because of A justifies nothing — the pair is a closed loop, and the link between them becomes one more thing to memorize. A real importance answer exits the loop: it names a consequence outside the pair.

## 4. Questions steer encoding

Self-generated questions are the steering interface of cognition: they direct attention, pre-commit relevance decisions, and create the purpose without which consumption does not encode. Studying produces learning only when the information consumed has a job to do.

- **Question form determines what gets built.** "Why does this matter?" forces evaluation against the big picture — highest yield. "How does X relate to Y?" probes specific relations — medium. "What is X?" retrieves an isolated fact — low, and at risk of collapsing into pure memorization. Because deep questions produce the facts as a side effect (the asymmetry above), the deep forms *replace* the shallow ones rather than supplementing them.
- **The cycle feeds itself.** Answering a real question generates material that generates deeper questions. This loop, not any single question, is what builds a topic out.
- **Two impostors.** A question whose answer is already known before asking has inquiry's form and does none of its work — the answer is retrieved, not discovered. And a genuine question *answered with a short, literal, closed reply* is wasted the other way: the answer existed to be explored for further relations, and closing it early forfeits that.
- **Unanswered is fine.** Hard questions do their encoding work in the attempt; a question that can't be answered yet can be parked without blocking anything.

## 5. Working memory is a budget

Cognitive load is finite. A piece of information with no frame forces the brain to juggle many open problems at once — what does this mean, why would I need it, how would I use it, how does it fit — and with that many live at once, focus and encoding both degrade. Knowing *why something matters* collapses most of those questions at a stroke; that is the mechanical reason importance-first learning feels different.

- **Two failure modes, symmetric.** Overload — more open at once than the budget covers — prevents encoding. Underload — no genuine difficulty — prevents engagement. The productive band sits between them, and it is not comfortable by default; for a learner with shallow habits, correct-feeling load registers as unfamiliar effort, not ease.
- **Raise, then offload.** Managed learning is a cycle, not load-avoidance: deep operations deliberately raise load; consolidating the result into external structure (the map, the grouped notes) offloads it, freeing budget for the next round. Offloading into an artifact is legitimate cognition, not a crutch — it is what makes the next round of deep processing possible. Short consolidation pauses *before* more input beat recovery after overload.
- **Priming spreads the load.** A short, low-detail pass before an intensive session builds the frame in advance, so the session's fire-hose of detail arrives into structure instead of void. Skipping this doesn't save the load; it concentrates all of it into the one session least able to carry it, and the shortfall compounds session over session.
- **Method overhead is real.** Any new way of studying itself consumes budget until practiced. Introduce one or two at a time; a learner running several unfamiliar techniques at once spends the whole budget on the techniques and none on the material.

## 6. Order is chosen, not given

The order in which information is processed is a free variable, decoupled from the order in which it is presented. This is the least intuitive claim in the model and one of the highest-leverage.

- **Sources are ordered for exposition.** An expert's knowledge has no inherent sequence — it is a non-linear network — but a book or lecture must serialize it somehow. That serialization is optimized for presenting, not encoding, and it typically front-loads detail. A learner who follows it commits the *teaching order* to memory instead of rebuilding the network that actually constitutes the expertise.
- **Global before local, at every scale.** Structure first, then parts inside it — within a sitting, across a week, across a study plan. Early passes broad and shallow; later passes add depth to a frame that already exists. Multi-pass coverage is also the safety net: a gap must survive every pass to survive at all, where a single linear pass misses silently.
- **Early structure is a hypothesis.** The first grouping exists to offload, not to be right; it should be revised freely while structure is forming. Revisability declines as detail accumulates — but it never reaches zero, and the decline is earned by having iterated honestly during the structural passes, not automatic.
- **The default failure is inheriting the frame.** Left alone, a learner's grouping mirrors the source's — and it feels like the only possible grouping ("there's no other way to organize this" is the signature symptom; there almost always is). The bias compounds: every session inside an inherited frame makes the next reorganization harder. Rigidly structured source material — protocols, checklists, formal taxonomies — resists independent reorganization hardest and needs the most deliberate effort. Pre-grouping before engaging the material is permissible only as a disposable springboard; a learner who can't bring themselves to revise the pre-group should stop pre-grouping entirely.

## 7. The illusions

Encoding cannot be observed directly, so learners audit it by feel — and feel lies in known directions. Any teaching setup must expect these:

- **Fluency ≠ knowledge.** Smooth re-reading, a lucid explanation received, material that "flows" — all produce confident familiarity with no production behind it. The smoother the input, the stronger the false signal.
- **The finished-artifact illusion.** Completing a map or a set of notes feels like the knowledge moved into the head; mostly the effort of transcription got mistaken for the effort of encoding. The artifact is scaffolding for encoding, not evidence of it.
- **Obvious-without-confusion.** A structure that feels obvious, arrived at without any pass through confusion, usually means the hard step was skipped — inherited from the source, or assembled by analysis with no evaluation. Real structural work passes through "this is uncomfortable and I'm not sure" on the way to "this is clear."
- **The feel-map is usable data.** Deep work feels effortful, slow at first, sometimes confusing — those are signals of the mechanism engaging, not of failure. Shallow work feels smooth, fast, and safe — that comfort is the warning. Teaching against the grain of these feelings requires *saying so out loud*: telling the learner which feelings mean what, so they stop steering by comfort.
